# How Do Local Governments Decide Whether Debt Is Affordable?

A local government can have legal authority to borrow and still decide that taking on more debt would put too much pressure on future budgets.

That distinction matters because **legal capacity and financial affordability are not the same question**.

A legal limit tells a government something about what it is permitted to do under the rules that apply to it. Affordability asks whether the resulting payments fit the community’s revenues, existing obligations, capital plan, service needs, and tolerance for financial risk.

There is no single debt ratio that answers that question for every city, county, district, or authority.

## Start with the future payment, not only the amount borrowed

The headline amount of a bond issue can attract attention, but the annual budget usually feels the borrowing through debt service over time.

A government considering new debt therefore needs to understand how principal and interest payments will fit into future budgets.

The same amount of debt can create different budget effects depending on the repayment structure, term, interest rate, and source of repayment.

GFOA recommends written debt management policies that address debt limits, structuring practices, issuance practices, and ongoing debt management. Those policies are meant to reflect the issuer’s specific legal and financial circumstances.

The useful question is not simply, “How much can we borrow?”

It is, “What future commitment are we creating, and how does that commitment fit the rest of the financial plan?”

## Legal limits are one guardrail

State constitutions, statutes, local charters, ordinances, bond documents, and voter approvals can all affect borrowing authority.

Those rules vary substantially.

A legal debt limit may be expressed in relation to assessed value or another measure. Some types of obligations may be treated differently from others. Certain borrowings may require voter approval while others may not.

Because of that variation, a legal limit should not be treated as a nationwide affordability standard.

A government can be below its legal limit and still face a difficult financial tradeoff if debt service would crowd out other priorities.

## Written debt policy adds local financial guardrails

GFOA’s debt management guidance says governments should develop criteria for when debt is appropriate and establish limits or acceptable ranges that reflect legal, public-policy, financial, and planning considerations.

A policy can look at measures such as debt service relative to revenue, debt relative to property value, debt per capita, or coverage ratios for revenue debt.

Those examples are not universal safe thresholds.

They are tools that an issuer may adapt to its own revenue structure, risks, capital needs, and legal environment.

The value of a written policy is consistency. It makes the government’s own decision rules visible before a particular project creates political or financial pressure.

## Existing obligations matter

New borrowing does not start from zero.

A government may already have outstanding bonds, leases, pensions, other long-term commitments, or debt scheduled to mature in future years.

The timing matters.

If older debt is about to be retired, the future repayment picture may look different from a situation in which existing obligations remain high for many years.

Readers should therefore look for a debt-service schedule rather than relying only on the total amount of outstanding debt.

MSRB guidance also emphasizes that the source of repayment differs by security structure. General obligation debt and revenue debt do not necessarily rely on the same resources.

That is another reason one ratio cannot tell the whole story.

## Capital planning belongs in the affordability decision

Debt often helps governments spread the cost of long-lived infrastructure over time.

That can align repayment with the useful life of an asset and with the residents who benefit from it.

But a capital plan can contain many projects competing for the same future financial capacity.

A government evaluating one bond issue should therefore consider the broader pipeline: roads, buildings, utilities, vehicles, technology, parks, or other assets that may require investment later.

Borrowing heavily for one project can reduce flexibility for another.

GFOA specifically connects debt management policy with long-term capital and financial planning.

## Revenue risk changes the answer

A government with stable, diverse recurring revenues may experience debt capacity differently from one that depends heavily on volatile or concentrated sources.

The same is true for revenue bonds. If repayment depends on a particular utility or enterprise revenue stream, the government must consider the strength and reliability of that pledged source and the legal terms governing it.

Economic conditions, tax-base concentration, rate-setting authority, service demand, and other local factors can all affect practical capacity.

Affordability is therefore a scenario question as much as a ratio question.

## Matching debt term to the asset matters

Debt policy often addresses maximum term and the relationship between repayment and the useful life of the financed asset.

The logic is straightforward: a government generally wants to avoid paying for an asset long after its public usefulness has ended.

That does not create one universal maturity rule. Different assets have different lives, legal constraints, financing structures, and replacement cycles.

The principle is to make the timing of the financing visible.

## Questions residents can ask

When a government proposes new borrowing, look for answers to questions such as:

1. What project or purpose will the debt finance?
2. What is the expected annual debt service?
3. Which revenue source is expected to make the payments?
4. What debt is already outstanding, and when does it mature?
5. What legal limits or voter-approval rules apply?
6. What limits or ranges are in the government’s written debt policy?
7. What other capital projects may need financing in the next several years?
8. Does the term of the debt make sense in relation to the asset being financed?

These questions do not produce a universal “safe” number.

They reveal the assumptions behind the decision.

## The practical takeaway

Debt affordability is not a judgment about whether borrowing is inherently good or bad.

It is a disciplined way to connect a long-term financing decision with future budget capacity.

Legal authority matters. So do annual debt service, existing obligations, revenue stability, capital needs, asset life, and written policy.

A strong affordability discussion makes those connections visible before the debt is issued.
