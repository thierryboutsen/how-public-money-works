# What Is a User Fee—and How Do Governments Decide What to Charge?

Why does a building permit have a fee?

Why might a recreation program charge participants while another public service is paid mostly from general taxes?

Why can two local governments charge different amounts for what appears to be a similar service?

The answer is not simply “because that is what the service costs.”

A **user fee** is generally tied to a service provided to a user, but setting the amount is a policy decision that can involve cost recovery, legal restrictions, affordability, market comparisons, program goals, and decisions about who should pay.

GFOA’s 2025 User Fee Policy best practice recommends that local governments adopt a policy identifying user fees and setting a clear strategy for them.

The value of that policy is transparency: the public should be able to understand why a particular fee exists and what the government is trying to accomplish with it.

## A user fee is connected to a service

For purposes of its best practice, GFOA defines user fees as charges collected in exchange for providing a service.

Examples include:

- utility fees;
- development fees;
- zoning fees;
- building-permit fees;
- transit fees;
- parks and recreation program fees.

That service connection matters.

GFOA distinguishes user fees from taxes or business-license charges that are not directly tied to a service being provided.

It is also important not to confuse user fees with fines or imposed penalties.

A fine is designed around enforcement or punishment, not ordinary service pricing.

If you want the broader distinction, see [Taxes, Fees, and Fines: What Is the Difference?](/taxes-fees-and-fines-difference).

## Governments may charge a fee for different reasons

Cost recovery is one reason to charge a user fee, but not the only reason.

A government might decide that people who directly use a service should pay some or all of its cost.

It might compare its fee with nearby governments or market alternatives.

It might intentionally subsidize part of the service because broader public benefits exist.

It might set a fee partly to influence demand or behavior.

GFOA recommends that the purpose and strategy for each fee be clearly defined.

That means the government should be able to explain not only **how much** it charges but **why**.

## Cost recovery requires defining the cost

“Recover the cost” sounds straightforward until the government has to define what the cost actually includes.

A service may involve:

- employee time;
- benefits;
- materials;
- vehicles or equipment;
- technology;
- facilities;
- administrative support;
- billing and collection;
- supervision;
- indirect overhead.

Different governments may allocate those costs differently depending on their accounting practices, policy goals, and legal requirements.

That is why GFOA recommends that, when cost recovery is the purpose, the policy should identify the cost-recovery goal and how costs are calculated.

The goal might be full recovery, partial recovery, or another defined approach.

There is no universal rule that every user fee must recover 100 percent of cost.

## Full cost recovery can conflict with other goals

A government may want to recover more of a service’s cost while also wanting people to use that service.

Those goals can conflict.

GFOA specifically warns that a high cost-recovery target can work against the purpose of the program in some cases.

A recreation program, transit service, document-access service, or other public program may have affordability, access, equity, or participation goals that influence the fee.

This does not mean every fee should be low.

It means the trade-off should be visible.

A strong policy explains which objective takes priority and why.

## Ability to pay and market comparisons can matter

GFOA’s best practice recognizes several possible strategies beyond pure cost recovery.

A government may consider:

- ability to pay;
- comparable prices charged by other governments;
- market rates;
- incentives or deterrents;
- service-access goals.

These factors do not automatically apply to every fee.

They are policy choices that should be documented.

For residents, this is an important clue: if the fee is lower than the estimated full cost, that difference may reflect an intentional subsidy rather than a calculation mistake.

If the fee is higher or structured differently, legal authority and program purpose should be examined before drawing conclusions.

## Laws and regulations can limit the options

User-fee authority varies by jurisdiction and type of service.

State law, local ordinances, charters, regulations, court decisions, grant conditions, or other legal rules may affect what can be charged, how the amount is calculated, and who must approve it.

GFOA therefore recommends documenting applicable laws and restrictions as part of the fee-policy process.

This is one reason the same fee cannot be evaluated with a universal formula.

The legal framework matters.

## Approval should match the government’s authority structure

Who approves the fee?

The answer can differ.

Some fees require formal governing-body action.

Others may be set administratively under delegated authority.

GFOA recommends that the user-fee policy be adopted at least at the level where the fees themselves are approved.

That helps connect the pricing strategy to the actual decision-making authority.

It also makes the policy easier for residents to find and understand.

## Fees should be reviewed, not forgotten

Costs change.

Demand changes.

Technology changes.

Laws change.

Program goals can change too.

A fee established years ago may no longer reflect the service or policy that exists today.

GFOA recommends that governments define how often fee policies and fees will be reviewed and updated.

Regular review does not mean every fee must increase.

It means the government should revisit whether the amount and strategy still make sense.

## A reader checklist

When you see a fee in a local budget or fee schedule, ask:

1. **What service is connected to the fee?**
2. **What is the stated purpose of charging it?**
3. **Is the goal full cost recovery, partial recovery, market comparison, affordability, behavior change, or something else?**
4. **How does the government calculate the underlying cost?**
5. **What laws or rules constrain the fee?**
6. **Who approves it?**
7. **How often is it reviewed?**
8. **Does the policy explain trade-offs between revenue and access to the service?**

Those questions reveal much more than the price alone.

## The practical takeaway

A user fee is not simply a small tax with another name.

It is a charge connected to a service, and its design should reflect a clear policy strategy.

Cost matters.

But so can affordability, access, market conditions, program goals, legal authority, and who benefits from the service.

A well-designed fee policy makes those choices visible.

That is what allows residents to evaluate whether the charge is consistent with the service and the government’s stated goals.
