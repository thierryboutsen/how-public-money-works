# How Local Governments Decide How Much Reserve Is Enough

“How much reserve should a local government keep?” sounds like a question that should have one clean percentage as an answer.

It does not.

Reserve adequacy depends on the government’s own revenue structure, spending risks, exposure to emergencies, access to other resources, financial policies, and ability to rebuild reserves after they are used.

That is why two well-managed local governments can reasonably choose different targets.

A useful reserve policy is not a competition to accumulate the largest balance. It is a framework for deciding how much protection is appropriate for the risks a government actually faces.

## Start with the purpose of reserves

Reserves give governments room to absorb events that are difficult to fit inside an ordinary annual budget.

Examples can include:

- a sudden revenue shortfall;
- a natural disaster or other emergency;
- an unexpected expenditure;
- a temporary economic downturn;
- a delay or reduction in intergovernmental aid;
- a transition while recurring revenues and expenditures are brought back into balance.

GFOA’s 2026 work on rethinking reserves describes reserves as protection against unplanned and unavoidable costs or losses when resources appropriated inside the budget are not sufficient.

That description is useful because it keeps the focus on **risk**, not on a single ratio.

## GFOA provides a reference point—but not a universal score

GFOA’s Fund Balance Guidelines for the General Fund recommends that general-purpose governments maintain, at a minimum, unrestricted budgetary fund balance in the general fund equal to no less than two months of regular general-fund operating revenues or regular general-fund operating expenditures.

That recommendation is widely cited.

It is also frequently oversimplified.

The same GFOA guidance says adequacy must reflect each government’s unique circumstances. It notes that some governments may reasonably need significantly more than the reference minimum, while certain large governments or states may operate differently because of scale, diversification, and predictability.

In other words, “two months” is a **best-practice reference point**, not a nationwide legal requirement and not a universal fiscal-health grade.

A local policy should explain why its chosen target fits the government’s circumstances.

## Revenue volatility changes the answer

A government whose major revenues are stable and predictable faces a different risk profile from one that depends heavily on volatile sources.

For example, a community highly dependent on a sales-sensitive or economically cyclical revenue source may experience sharper swings during a downturn.

A government that relies significantly on state or federal aid may face another type of uncertainty if those transfers can be reduced or delayed.

GFOA specifically identifies revenue predictability as a factor in determining reserve adequacy.

The question for readers is not simply:

**What percentage is in reserve?**

It is:

**How uncertain are the revenues that must support essential services?**

Greater volatility can justify a larger financial cushion.

## Expenditure risk matters too

Revenue risk is only one side of the equation.

Some governments face unusually volatile or concentrated expenditure risks.

Examples might include:

- exposure to hurricanes, wildfires, floods, earthquakes, or severe weather;
- aging infrastructure that could require major emergency repairs;
- unusually large one-time legal, operational, or capital needs;
- responsibility for services that cannot be reduced quickly during a downturn.

GFOA’s guidance specifically points to exposure to significant one-time outlays as a factor in reserve policy.

A government with stable revenues but unusually high emergency exposure may therefore choose a higher reserve target than a superficially similar government.

## Access to other resources can change the required cushion

A reserve target should not be read in isolation from the rest of the financial structure.

A government may have:

- resources available in other funds;
- legally restricted contingency resources;
- insurance coverage;
- access to liquidity;
- borrowing capacity;
- emergency aid mechanisms;
- other financial tools that influence how much pressure falls on the general fund.

Those resources are not automatically substitutes for general-fund reserves, and some may not be available quickly or legally for the same purpose.

But they belong in the analysis.

GFOA’s fund-balance guidance explicitly notes that resources in other funds can be relevant when assessing the adequacy of unrestricted general-fund balance.

## A policy should define both a floor and a response

A reserve policy becomes more useful when it does more than state a target percentage.

GFOA’s Financial Foundations for Budgeting describes the value of boundaries such as a reserve floor and ceiling. If reserves fall outside that range, the policy should point toward a response.

That response matters because the reserve target is only one part of the system.

A good policy can describe:

- the target or acceptable range;
- circumstances that permit use;
- authorization requirements;
- how quickly the government should begin rebuilding;
- sources that may be used for replenishment;
- what happens when balances are above the policy range.

A number without a use-and-replenishment framework tells only part of the story.

## Replenishment is part of reserve adequacy

A government can have a strong reserve today and a weak reserve strategy tomorrow if it has no realistic way to rebuild after use.

GFOA recommends that policies specify how fund balance will be replenished when it falls below the prescribed level. Its guidance says governments should generally seek to replenish fund balances within one to three years of use, while recognizing that the appropriate horizon depends on the circumstances.

That is another reason not to judge reserve health from one year alone.

A reserve draw during a major emergency may be exactly what the policy was designed for.

The important follow-up questions are:

- Why was it used?
- Was the use consistent with policy?
- What recurring changes are needed afterward?
- Is there a credible replenishment plan?

## Bigger is not automatically better

A very large reserve can reduce risk, but resources held in reserve are also resources not currently being used for another public purpose.

GFOA’s newer reserve work explicitly encourages governments to think beyond a simple “bigger is better” assumption and to optimize reserves for their circumstances.

That does not mean large reserves are wrong.

It means the government should be able to explain the policy logic.

If reserves materially exceed the adopted target after foreseeable risks have been considered, readers can reasonably ask what the policy says about those excess resources.

If reserves are below target, readers can ask how and when the government plans to rebuild them.

The quality of the explanation matters as much as the raw percentage.

## How to read a reserve policy

When reviewing a local government’s reserve policy, ask:

1. **What balance is being measured?** Unrestricted fund balance, unassigned fund balance, a named reserve, or something else?
2. **What is the target?** A minimum, a range, a floor and ceiling, or another structure?
3. **Why was that target chosen?** Does the policy discuss revenue volatility, emergency exposure, aid dependence, or other risks?
4. **When can reserves be used?**
5. **Who authorizes the use?**
6. **How will the balance be replenished?**
7. **What happens if reserves are above the target?**
8. **Does the government use long-term forecasts to test the policy under different conditions?**

These questions help turn a reserve ratio into a financial-management story.

## The practical takeaway

There is no single percentage that proves every local government has “enough” reserves.

GFOA provides an important reference point for many general-purpose governments, but its own guidance makes clear that reserve adequacy depends on circumstances and should be evaluated with long-term forecasting and risk analysis.

The strongest reserve policies connect four things:

**risk + target + rules for use + replenishment**

That framework is more useful than treating one percentage as a universal answer.
