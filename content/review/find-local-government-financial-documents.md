# How to Find and Understand Your Local Government's Financial Documents

The hardest part of reading local public finance is often not the accounting. It is finding the right document.

A finance page may contain proposed budgets, adopted budgets, amendments, monthly reports, annual reports, audits, capital plans, and meeting packets. Similar names can cover different entities and years. A reliable search begins with five anchors: question, government, period, document, and version.

## Define the question before searching

“Where did the money go?” is too broad for one PDF. Turn it into a document question.

What was planned? Look for a proposed or adopted budget. What changed? Look for amendments and monitoring reports. What happened after year-end? Find the annual financial report and audit. Which projects are planned? Open the capital improvement plan. Who approved a decision? Search agendas, staff reports, minutes, ordinances, and resolutions. How is debt repaid? Look for official statements and financial-report notes.

The document follows the question—not the other way around.

## Identify the correct government

A service may involve a city, county, school district, special district, state agency, or regional authority. Search the official entity name, not only the place name.

Confirm that the website belongs to the government. Official domains, state filing portals, and links from recognized government pages are stronger starting points than an unlabeled copy on a third-party site.

If several entities share a service, find each one's role. One may own the asset, another administer a grant, and another provide local matching resources.

## Confirm fiscal period and document status

Calendar year, fiscal year, and biennium are not interchangeable. Check the beginning and ending dates printed in the document.

Then verify status. Proposed, recommended, approved, adopted, amended, preliminary, unaudited, and final describe different stages. A search result may rank an old proposed version above the current adopted one.

Record the publication or meeting date and download the file from its official page when possible. File names alone may omit revisions.

## Use a practical document map

- **Adopted budget:** planned resources, appropriations, services, and policy for the budget period.
- **Budget amendment or supplemental budget:** authorized changes after adoption.
- **Monitoring report:** activity during the period, often compared with plan.
- **Annual financial report:** reported financial results after the period.
- **Audit report:** independent conclusions within a stated scope.
- **Capital improvement plan:** multi-year project priorities and funding assumptions.
- **Agenda packet and minutes:** proposals, supporting analysis, public meeting, and formal action.
- **Debt disclosure:** issuer, purpose, security, repayment, and continuing information.

Not every government uses these exact names or publishes each item in one place.

## Search more precisely

On the government site, try the entity name plus a document term and fiscal year. If site navigation is difficult, a search engine query with `site:` and the official domain can narrow results. Useful terms include “adopted budget,” “annual financial report,” “audit,” “capital improvement plan,” “budget amendment,” “agenda packet,” and “official statement.”

State auditor, comptroller, treasurer, or transparency portals may host filings. The Washington State Auditor, for example, provides annual filing and reporting resources. The Texas Comptroller describes public access to budgets and annual financial reports in its local transparency guidance. These are examples, not a promise that every state uses one portal.

## Cross-check rather than trust one summary

A summary helps orientation. Use the full document for definitions, scope, footnotes, and limitations. Compare an adopted budget with amendments and the final report. Compare an audit headline with the opinion and findings. Compare a project page with the capital plan and governing-body action.

When two amounts differ, confirm entity, period, fund, accounting basis, and version before assuming an error.

The [city budget guide](/what-is-a-city-budget-and-why-should-you-care) offers a foundation for the first document in that chain.

## Keep a small evidence record

Save the official page URL, document title, entity, fiscal period, status, retrieval date, and relevant page numbers. That record makes your question reproducible and protects against later link changes.

If a required record is unavailable, use the government's records-request process or contact the clerk or finance office. Procedures and legal rights vary, so consult the applicable official guidance.

## A good search is part of financial literacy

Understanding public money begins before the first table. It begins by selecting the authoritative record that answers the question you actually have.
