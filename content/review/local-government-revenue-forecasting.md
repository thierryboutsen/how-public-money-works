# How Local Governments Forecast Revenue—and Why Forecasts Change

A revenue forecast can look deceptively precise.

A budget may show projected property-tax revenue, sales-tax revenue, permits, service charges, state aid, federal grants, and other sources down to the dollar.

But a forecast is not a promise.

It is an estimate built from what is known today about the economy, tax base, laws, policy choices, historical collections, administrative timing, and other factors that influence how much money may actually arrive.

That is why a forecast can change during the budget process without the earlier estimate having been dishonest or careless.

The important question is not whether the number changed.

It is **why** it changed and whether the assumptions behind the revision are transparent.

## Forecasting starts with the revenues that matter

GFOA recommends that governments forecast major revenues and expenditures and integrate those forecasts into the budget process.

Different revenue sources behave differently.

Property-tax revenue may be influenced by assessed values, levy rules, exemptions, collection rates, and timing.

Sales-related revenue may react more quickly to consumer activity and economic conditions.

Permit revenue may rise or fall with construction and development.

Intergovernmental revenue may depend on state or federal formulas, appropriations, grants, or policy decisions.

A useful forecast begins by identifying which revenue sources matter most and what actually drives them.

A single forecasting method is not automatically best for every source.

## Historical data is a starting point, not the whole answer

Past collections are useful because they reveal trends, seasonality, volatility, and unusual events.

But simply extending a line from the past can be misleading.

Suppose a city collected unusually high permit revenue during a construction boom. If development slows, treating that peak as the new normal could overstate future revenue.

The reverse can also happen. A temporary disruption can make historical results look weaker than the underlying revenue base.

That is why forecasters combine historical data with information about the conditions that produced those numbers.

GFOA’s forecasting guidance emphasizes both quantitative information and professional judgment.

The point is not to replace data with intuition.

It is to understand the forces behind the data.

## Assumptions are where the forecast becomes understandable

A forecast is much easier to evaluate when its assumptions are visible.

GFOA recommends that governments clearly state the assumptions and methodology behind forecasts and make them available to stakeholders.

Assumptions might address:

- expected economic growth or slowdown;
- assessed-value changes;
- inflation;
- employment or consumer activity;
- policy changes;
- new development;
- collection rates;
- changes in state or federal funding;
- one-time events that should not be repeated.

The assumptions should be connected to objective information where possible.

GFOA’s current budget-process guidance also recommends comparing key assumptions with credible third-party forecasts or analyses when practical.

Transparency matters because a forecast can appear objective while still depending on important judgment calls.

## Conservative and objective forecasts involve trade-offs

Some governments intentionally use a conservative forecasting policy.

A conservative forecast may estimate revenue cautiously to reduce the risk of a shortfall.

Other governments may aim for the most objective estimate of the expected outcome.

Neither approach eliminates uncertainty.

GFOA notes that a government should be transparent about its forecasting policy because the choice affects budget decisions.

A forecast that is too optimistic can support spending that later becomes difficult to sustain.

A forecast that is excessively conservative can also have consequences if it systematically understates resources that could support services.

The useful question is whether the approach is explicit, consistent, and tied to the government’s risk tolerance.

## Why forecasts change during the budget process

Conditions do not stop changing while a budget is being prepared.

A forecast may be revised because:

- actual collections came in above or below expectations;
- a major economic indicator changed;
- a new law affected a revenue source;
- a grant or transfer amount became clearer;
- a development project accelerated or was delayed;
- a one-time event was identified;
- better data became available.

GFOA recommends that forecasts be regularly monitored and periodically updated.

Its current budget-process guidance goes further: when key assumptions change, governments should be prepared to update the forecast and financial plan and share the new information with decision-makers.

A revision is therefore not automatically evidence that the first estimate was poor.

Sometimes it is evidence that the process is responding to new information.

## Uncertainty should influence the budget, not disappear from it

A forecast gives decision-makers a working estimate.

It does not remove uncertainty.

That uncertainty matters because recurring spending decisions may be difficult to reverse quickly.

If a highly uncertain revenue estimate is used to support permanent commitments, the government may face pressure later if collections fall short.

This connects directly to structural balance.

A budget is stronger when recurring spending is supported by recurring revenue that can reasonably be expected to continue.

See [What Is a Structurally Balanced Budget?](/structurally-balanced-budget-explained) and [One-Time Revenue: Why Governments Should Be Careful Using It for Ongoing Costs](/one-time-revenue-ongoing-costs).

## Forecast ranges and scenarios can improve the conversation

Not every government presents a single point estimate in the same way.

Some may use alternative scenarios, ranges, sensitivity analysis, or stress tests to show how revenue could change under different assumptions.

Those tools can help decision-makers see the consequences of uncertainty before adopting the budget.

They are especially useful when important revenue sources are volatile or exposed to economic conditions outside the government’s control.

The goal is not to predict every possible future.

It is to make the important risks visible.

## Five questions residents can ask

When reading a revenue forecast, look beyond the final number.

Ask:

1. **What are the major revenue sources?**
2. **What assumptions drive the forecast?**
3. **What historical data or outside information supports those assumptions?**
4. **Which revenue sources are most uncertain or volatile?**
5. **What changed when the forecast was revised?**

You can also ask whether the budget explains what would happen if revenue comes in below forecast.

A forecast becomes more useful when it is connected to decisions, not presented as an isolated table.

## The practical takeaway

Revenue forecasting is not a promise about the future.

It is a disciplined way to make decisions under uncertainty.

A strong process combines historical information, revenue-specific drivers, documented assumptions, professional judgment, periodic updates, and clear communication.

Forecasts should change when material facts or assumptions change.

The public should be able to see the reason for the change.

That is the difference between a forecast that merely produces a number and one that improves financial decision-making.
