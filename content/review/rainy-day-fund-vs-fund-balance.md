# What Is a Rainy Day Fund—and How Is It Different from Fund Balance?

A city says it has a healthy fund balance. Another page in the budget says the city also has a rainy-day fund. A third document shows cash in the bank.

Those numbers may be related, but they do not necessarily describe the same thing.

That distinction matters because residents can easily hear “fund balance” and assume all of it is emergency money. They can also hear “rainy-day fund” and assume it must be a completely separate bank account. Neither assumption is reliable across all governments.

A better way to read the documents is to ask three separate questions:

1. What financial position is being reported?
2. What portion of those resources is constrained or designated for a particular purpose?
3. What policy governs when a reserve can be used and how it should be rebuilt?

## Fund balance is a financial-reporting and budgetary concept

For governmental funds, **fund balance** describes the difference between a fund’s assets and deferred outflows and its liabilities and deferred inflows under the applicable accounting framework. In budget practice, governments also use the term to describe available financial resources on a budgetary basis.

The Government Finance Officers Association (GFOA) warns that readers should understand the difference between GAAP fund balance and budgetary fund balance rather than treating them as automatically identical.

GASB Statement No. 54 organizes reported governmental fund balance into five classifications based on the strength and source of constraints on how resources can be used:

- nonspendable;
- restricted;
- committed;
- assigned;
- unassigned.

Those classifications tell readers something about constraints. They do not by themselves tell you that a particular amount is a “rainy-day fund.”

That is why the phrase **fund balance** should not be translated mentally into “money sitting there for emergencies.”

## A rainy-day fund is primarily a reserve-policy concept

“Rainy-day fund” is a familiar public-finance phrase for resources intentionally held back to help absorb future shocks. Governments may use other labels, such as stabilization reserve, emergency reserve, contingency reserve, or general-fund reserve.

GFOA’s 2026 work on rethinking reserves describes reserves as resources available outside the current budget to provide protection when appropriated resources are not enough for unplanned or unavoidable costs or losses.

The important point is purpose.

A reserve policy can define:

- the target level or range;
- circumstances that justify use;
- who can authorize use;
- whether some events are excluded;
- the time horizon for replenishment;
- how amounts above or below policy should be handled.

A reserve therefore reflects a **policy decision about financial resilience**. Fund balance is the broader financial position in which some of those reserve resources may be reported.

## They can overlap without being identical

A local government may designate part of its general-fund resources for stabilization. Depending on the legal and policy structure, those resources could appear within committed, assigned, restricted, or other relevant fund-balance classifications.

Another government might maintain a separate fund or legally distinct arrangement for a similar purpose.

That is why there is no universal accounting shortcut such as:

**rainy-day fund = total fund balance**

or

**rainy-day fund = unassigned fund balance**

The correct relationship depends on the government’s accounting structure, governing actions, legal constraints, and adopted policy.

GFOA’s fund-balance guidance also notes that resources in other funds can matter when evaluating the adequacy of general-fund reserves. In some circumstances, restricted resources may function as a contingency resource for analytical purposes. Context matters.

## Cash is another different concept

Fund balance is not simply the cash balance of a checking account.

A government may have cash that belongs to multiple funds, cash committed to obligations, receivables not yet collected, liabilities that will require cash later, or timing differences between revenue collection and expenditures.

A rainy-day policy therefore cannot be evaluated by looking at one bank balance alone.

For readers, the safest approach is to treat **cash**, **fund balance**, and **policy reserves** as related but distinct concepts.

You can read more about this distinction in [What Does Fund Balance Mean—and Is It Available to Spend?](/what-does-fund-balance-mean).

## What makes a reserve policy useful?

A named reserve becomes much more informative when the policy explains what the name actually means.

GFOA recommends a formal policy for the level of unrestricted fund balance maintained in the general fund. The policy should provide a framework for increasing or decreasing that level over time and explain how depleted resources will be replenished.

A useful policy helps answer practical questions.

### When can the reserve be used?

A policy may identify events such as severe revenue shortfalls, major emergencies, disasters, or other exceptional conditions.

That is different from using reserves as a routine recurring revenue source.

### How low can it go?

Some policies establish a floor, a target, a range, or a combination of those concepts.

The number should be interpreted together with the risks the government faces rather than as a universal score.

### How is it replenished?

If reserves fall below policy, the plan should explain how and over what period they will be restored.

GFOA’s fund-balance guidance emphasizes replenishment because a reserve that is used repeatedly without rebuilding gradually loses its ability to protect the government from the next shock.

## Why terminology can be confusing

Local governments do not all use the same labels.

One government may use “rainy-day fund” in public communication while its formal financial statements show the amount through fund-balance classifications. Another may use “budget stabilization reserve.” A third may have several reserves for different risks.

The legal form can also vary by state and locality.

That is why it is risky to compare two governments solely by the label printed on a chart.

The useful comparison is what the resources are for, how they are constrained, when they may be used, and how they are rebuilt.

## A reader checklist

When you see “rainy-day fund,” “reserve,” or “fund balance” in a local document, look for:

1. **The accounting classification.** Is the amount restricted, committed, assigned, unassigned, or described differently?
2. **The governing policy.** Is there a written reserve or fund-balance policy?
3. **The purpose.** What type of shock or need is the reserve designed to address?
4. **The authorization rule.** Who can approve use?
5. **The target or range.** Does the government explain why that level fits its risks?
6. **The replenishment rule.** What happens after the reserve is used?
7. **Other available resources.** Are there additional funds or legally constrained resources that affect the overall picture?

The goal is not to find the biggest number.

It is to understand what each number represents.

## The practical takeaway

Fund balance answers a broad question about the financial position of a governmental fund.

A rainy-day fund or reserve answers a policy question about which resources are intentionally protected for future risk and under what conditions they can be used.

Sometimes the two overlap substantially. Sometimes they do not.

The documents that connect them are the government’s financial statements, budget, and reserve policy.

Reading those together gives a much clearer picture than treating every fund-balance dollar as emergency savings.
