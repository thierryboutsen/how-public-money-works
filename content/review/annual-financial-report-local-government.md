# Annual Financial Reports: What They Show After the Budget Year Ends

A local government may publish an adopted budget, a year-end financial report, an audit report, and several shorter summaries. The names can make these documents sound interchangeable. They are not.

The adopted budget is mainly a plan and an authorization for a future period. An annual financial report looks backward and presents financial information for a period that has ended. Reading the two together can be useful, but only after you understand what each document was designed to do.

## The budget and the annual report answer different questions

An adopted budget asks: What does the government expect to collect, what does it plan to spend, and what spending authority has been approved?

An annual financial report asks: What financial activity was reported after the year ended, what resources and obligations were recorded, and what information is needed to understand those results?

That difference matters. A budget may include forecasts, policy choices, staffing plans, service goals, and appropriations. A financial report follows accounting and reporting rules. Its presentation may use a different basis of accounting or a different grouping of funds than the budget.

This is why a number in the adopted budget may not match a number that appears to describe the same activity in the annual report. The difference is not automatically an error. It may reflect timing, classification, scope, or accounting basis. The reconciliation or notes should help explain it.

## A practical map of the report

Report titles and layouts vary, but several components commonly help a resident find their footing.

The introductory material identifies the government, reporting period, and organization of the report. Management's discussion and analysis, when included, gives an overview of major changes and explains some of the reasons behind them. It is a useful entry point, but it is management's explanation—not a substitute for the statements and notes.

The basic financial statements present the government's reported financial position and activity. Fund statements then separate information by accounting fund, allowing readers to see resources that are tracked for different purposes. Notes explain policies, definitions, commitments, debt, pensions, and other matters that the face of a statement cannot show clearly.

Some reports also include budget-to-actual schedules, statistical sections, or introductory summaries. A shorter popular annual financial report may be a helpful orientation when one exists, but the complete official report remains the source for details.

## Why the report can show two views of the same government

Government-wide statements and fund statements are not duplicate pages. They use different perspectives.

The government-wide view is designed to show the government more broadly, including longer-term assets and obligations for covered activities. Governmental fund statements focus more narrowly on current financial resources and how those resources move through individual funds.

Neither view is the single "real" number. Each answers a different question. A reader looking for the cost of long-lived infrastructure may need the broader view. A reader asking how much remained in a particular governmental fund may need the fund statements and notes.

Avoid adding balances from different statements unless the report explicitly tells you they are comparable. Reconciliations exist because the perspectives differ.

## How to compare budget and reported results carefully

Start with the reporting period. Confirm that both documents cover the same fiscal year and the same governmental entity. Then identify which funds or activities the comparison includes.

Next, look for the budget-to-actual presentation. It may show the original budget, the final amended budget, actual amounts on the budgetary basis, and a variance. Read the headings closely. "Final budget" may include amendments made during the year, so it is not necessarily the same as the budget first adopted.

Then read the note describing the budgetary basis. If the budget and financial statements use different recognition rules or scopes, the report may include a reconciliation. That explanation is more reliable than assuming every difference represents overspending or underspending.

A variance can lead to a useful question: Was it caused by timing, lower demand, delayed hiring, a project schedule, a revenue estimate, or a policy change? The report may explain the reason, but sometimes a budget monitoring report or meeting record provides more detail.

## Five questions for a first reading

You do not need to read every page in order. Begin with five questions:

1. What government and fiscal period does this report cover?
2. Which statements are government-wide and which are fund statements?
3. Where is the budget-to-actual comparison, and what basis does it use?
4. Which changes does management identify as significant?
5. Which notes explain debt, restrictions, commitments, or unusual items relevant to your question?

Write down the page numbers that answer each question. That small map makes later reading much faster.

## What the report cannot establish by itself

An annual financial report is not a complete scorecard for service quality. Spending an authorized amount does not prove that a program achieved its goal. Spending less does not automatically mean efficiency, and spending more does not automatically mean waste.

The report also does not eliminate the need to understand local structure. Cities, counties, school districts, and special districts may issue separate reports, use different fund arrangements, and operate under different legal requirements. A report for one entity does not describe every public dollar in the community.

Finally, an audited report does not mean every transaction or operational decision was tested. The audit opinion and scope must be read on their own terms.

## Use the report as a bridge, not a verdict

The most productive approach is to connect documents: the adopted budget shows planned authority, monitoring reports show changes during the year, and the annual financial report shows reported results after the period ends.

Together, they help a resident ask better questions. What changed? Why did it change? Which fund was affected? Was the change temporary? Where will follow-up appear?
