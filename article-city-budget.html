What Is a City Budget — and Why Should You Care?

A plain-language walk through the document that shapes your streets, your schools, and your local services — and how to read one without a degree in accounting.
By
Eliana Faria Lima
Senior Practice, Public Finance
8 min read 1,920 words March MMXXVI
Editorial illustration · placeholder for documentary photograph
A budget is a public document. The question is not whether it can be read; it is whether it will be.

Every year, your city decides how to spend hundreds of millions of dollars — on streets, schools, libraries, parks, fire trucks, water lines, animal control, building inspections, and a long list of other things. That decision is called a budget.

A budget is not a technical document for accountants. It is the most important public document your city produces in any given year. It is, in plain terms, a statement of priorities written in numbers — a written answer to the question, what do we, together, choose to pay for?

And yet most people never read one. Budgets are long. They are full of unfamiliar vocabulary. They are usually published as a PDF, with no real introduction, on a city website. Even motivated citizens often open a budget document, scroll a few pages, and close it again.

The purpose of this essay is to make that act less intimidating. We will not learn government accounting. We will learn just enough to read a budget the way a citizen needs to — with patience, and with the right questions.

§ I. What a budget actually is

A city budget is two things at once. It is a plan: a public statement of what the city intends to do in the coming year, and what those things will cost. And it is a permission: once it is adopted, it is the legal authority the city government has to spend public money. Without an adopted budget, in most American cities, the government cannot operate.

Key Term
Appropriation

A legal authorization, set by the city council, that allows a specific amount of money to be spent on a specific purpose during a specific period — usually one fiscal year.

That word — appropriation — is the workhorse of public finance. When you read a budget, almost every dollar in it is an appropriation: a permission, granted by the elected body, for the government to spend on something the public has agreed to fund.

§ II. Where the money comes from

Before you can spend, you have to raise. Every city budget starts with a revenue side: the money the city expects to receive in the coming year. For most American cities, that revenue comes from a familiar handful of sources.

  1. Property taxes, paid by people who own land or buildings inside the city.
  2. Sales taxes and other local taxes, collected when residents or visitors buy things.
  3. Fees and charges — for permits, parking, water service, garbage collection, building inspections, and many other things the city does.
  4. Transfers from state or federal government, often tied to specific programs.
  5. Borrowing, typically through municipal bonds, for long-term investments like roads or buildings.

A useful first question, when you open a budget, is therefore simply: where does this city's money come from? The answer often surprises new readers. The mix is rarely what people assume, and it varies a great deal from city to city.

A Reader's Diagram

The Two Sides of a Budget

A budget can always be read as two columns. The left one is what the city expects to receive. The right is what it intends to spend. They must, by law, balance.

§ III. Where the money goes

The other side of the budget — the expenditure side — is where most readers find the questions they actually care about. How much do we spend on public safety? On parks? On schools? How much goes to administration? How much is paying off debt from past projects?

Cities organize this side in different ways. Some group it by department: police, fire, public works, parks, planning, finance. Others organize it by function: public safety, transportation, culture, general government. A few group it by program: street paving, after-school programs, food inspections, library hours.

It is worth knowing, when you read your own city's budget, which of these structures it uses. Once you do, you can read almost any line in the document with the same simple question — what is this paying for, and is it the right amount?

A budget is the most honest piece of writing a city produces. Whatever the speeches say, the budget says what was actually done.

§ IV. Why you should care

Here is the practical answer, in three parts.

First, the budget affects you directly. The fire department that responds to a call on your block is funded in the budget. The library hours, the park you walk to, the speed at which your street is repaved after a winter — all of it is a line, somewhere, in this document.

Second, the budget is where political promises become real. Politicians say many things in campaign season. The budget shows which of those things were funded, at what level, and which were quietly set aside.

Third, the budget is the document where your voice is, by law, allowed to be heard. Most American cities hold a public hearing — sometimes several — before the budget is adopted. Anyone, including you, can speak. Very few people do. The result is that the citizens who do show up have, in practice, a great deal of influence over what gets funded and what gets cut.

§ V. How to read one, in practice

You do not need to read a budget cover to cover. A useful first reading takes about an hour, and it goes roughly like this.

  1. Find the budget message or executive summary — usually the first ten pages. The mayor or city manager explains the year's priorities in plain language.
  2. Find the revenue summary. Note the three or four biggest sources of money. Ask: are any of them growing? Are any falling?
  3. Find the expenditure summary by department or function. Note the five biggest spending areas.
  4. Compare to last year. Most budgets show the prior year next to the current one. The differences — what went up, what went down — tell the real story.
  5. Note one thing that surprises you. Write down the question. Bring it to the next public hearing, or send it to your council member.

That is the entire method. It will not make you an accountant. It will make you a reader of public finance — which is, in any community, a small and useful minority.

§ VI. A final note

Public money is, in the simplest and most literal sense, our money — pooled together, given over to a small number of people we elect, and spent on things we have decided, together, we want as a community. The budget is the document where that decision is written down.

If you read your city's budget once a year, with patience and the right questions, you will know more about how your community actually works than ninety-nine percent of your neighbors. That is not a small civic achievement.

It is, in fact, what democracy is for.
